## Q1. Validated study-habit / self-regulated-learning instruments useful for planning

### Takeaway
The best construct anchors for a study-plan survey are the MSLQ resource-management subscales ("Time and Study Environment", 8 items; "Effort Regulation", 4 items), the procrastination construct (Steel's 12-item Pure Procrastination Scale) and chronotype (the 5-item reduced MEQ or the MCTQ mid-sleep approach). LASSI is a commercial product that is sold per administration, so its items must not be copied into a free app. For the other instruments, these searches could not confirm whether reuse is allowed. The safest approach is to write original items that are *informed by* each construct, cite the instrument as the conceptual source, and avoid calling the result "the MSLQ" or a "validated" scale.

### Cited Findings
**MSLQ (Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire; Pintrich, Smith, Garcia & McKeachie, 1991)**
- The manual is ERIC document ED338122 ("A Manual for the Use of the Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire"). The questionnaire is reported to be on pp. 41–48 of the manual. — [ProQuest/ERIC listing](https://www.proquest.com/docview/62892620); [ERIC ED338122](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED338122)
- 81 items in 15 scales: 6 motivation scales (31 items) and 9 learning-strategy scales (31 cognitive/metacognitive items plus 19 resource-management items). — [Psicologia: Reflexão e Crítica 2023, doi:10.1186/s41155-023-00280-0](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s41155-023-00280-0); [EdInstruments MSLQ](https://edinstruments.org/instruments/motivated-strategies-learning-questionnaire-mslq)
- "Time and Study Environment" has 8 items and covers managing time and choosing or organizing a suitable place to study. "Effort Regulation" has 4 items and covers the student's commitment and ability to control effort and attention during learning. Both belong to the resource-management group, together with peer learning and help-seeking. — [CBE–Life Sciences Education 2018 table via PMC6234818](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6234818/table/T2)
- Response format: a 7-point scale from "not at all true of me" to "very true of me". This was seen in a Scribd copy of the manual, so verify it against the ERIC original. — [Scribd copy of MSLQ manual](https://www.scribd.com/document/495681273/Ms-Lq-Manual)
- The MSLQ exists in 11 versions/translations. — [EdInstruments node 561](https://edinstruments.org/node/561)
- **Licensing:** None of the search results stated the MSLQ's copyright or reuse terms. Being hosted on ERIC does not by itself put a document in the public domain. — (search returned no licensing statement; see Gaps)

**LASSI (Learning and Study Strategies Inventory, 3rd ed.; Weinstein, Palmer & Acee, 2016)**
- 60 items across 10 scales (6 items each): Anxiety, Attitude, Concentration, Information Processing, Motivation, Selecting Main Ideas, Self Testing, Test Strategies, Time Management, Using Academic Resources. It takes about 9–11 minutes. — [Buros/UNL listing](https://marketplace.unl.edu/buros/learning-and-study-strategies-inventory-third-edition.html); [EdInstruments LASSI](https://edinstruments.org/instruments/learning-and-study-strategies-inventory-3rd-edition-lassi)
- Published by H&H Publishing (Florida) and sold per instrument. One third-party listing gives about $4.50 per administration and site licences (price not verified at the publisher). — [Buros/UNL listing](https://marketplace.unl.edu/buros/learning-and-study-strategies-inventory-third-edition.html); [Lamar University "Access the LASSI"](https://www.lamar.edu/center-for-academic-success/_files/documents/access-the-lassi.pdf)
- **Licensing:** LASSI is commercial and proprietary. Do not reproduce its items. Its scale *names* (e.g., Time Management, Concentration) can be cited as constructs.

**Pure Procrastination Scale (PPS; Steel, 2010)**
- Steel, P. (2010). Arousal, avoidant and decisional procrastinators: Do they exist? *Personality and Individual Differences*, 48, 926–934. — [Author PDF via Psychology Today](https://cdn2.psychologytoday.com/assets/attachments/49705/arousal-avoidant-and-decisional-procrastinators-do-they-exist.pdf)
- The PPS has 12 items taken from earlier scales: DPQ (items 1–3), GPS (4–8) and AIP (9–12). Items were selected by factor analysis of responses from more than 4,000 respondents. It uses a 5-point scale. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2017, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01927](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01927/abstract); [PMC5676095](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5676095)
- **Licensing:** The search found no statement. The PPS items come from older third-party scales (Lay's GPS, McCown's AIP, Mann's DPQ), so copyright may sit with several parties. — (unverified; see Gaps)

**Tuckman Procrastination Scale (Tuckman, 1991)**
- The scale exists and is cited as Tuckman (1991). One Spanish validation study reported better psychometric properties for it than for the alternatives compared. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2022, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2022.784471](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.784471/pdf)
- The item count ("16 items") and licensing could **not** be verified in these searches.

**Chronotype: MEQ, rMEQ and MCTQ**
- MEQ (Horne & Östberg, 1976) is described as the first validated chronotype instrument. The reduced MEQ (rMEQ; Adan & Almirall, 1991, *Personality and Individual Differences*) has 5 items and was created because the full MEQ was too long for some settings. — [Univ. Barcelona repository PDF](https://diposit.ub.edu/dspace/bitstream/2445/220392/1/844004.pdf); [PMC2988284](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2988284)
- MCTQ (Roenneberg, Wirz-Justice & Merrow, 2003, *J Biol Rhythms*) estimates chronotype from one reference point: the midpoint of sleep on free days (MSF), computed from reported sleep and wake times. — [Univ. Augsburg repository PDF](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/83600/83600.pdf); [UPenn CBT-I copy of the MCTQ](https://www.med.upenn.edu/cbti/assets/user-content/documents/Munich%20Chronotype%20Questionnaire%20(MCTQ).pdf)
- **Licensing:** No terms-of-use statement for the MEQ, rMEQ or MCTQ came up in the searches. — (see Gaps)

### Inferences
- **Recommended mapping of constructs to plan decisions** (my synthesis, not from a source):
  - Time management / study environment (MSLQ TSE; LASSI Time Management) → usual study place, noise and device distractions, whether the student already uses a schedule → plan structure and environment tips.
  - Effort regulation (MSLQ ER) → "When work is boring, I keep going / I give up" → session length and break frequency (e.g., start with shorter blocks).
  - Procrastination (PPS) → 1–2 items on starting late or delaying → earlier start reminders and small "first step" tasks.
  - Chronotype (rMEQ / MCTQ logic) → "When do you feel most alert?" plus usual school-night and weekend sleep/wake times → which time slots to suggest. The MCTQ's mid-sleep-on-free-days idea can be computed from 2 time questions without copying MCTQ wording.
  - Practical constraints (not from any instrument): available days/times, upcoming tests, subjects, preferred session length.
- Because the app plans after 3–4 sessions, a self-report item can be checked against observed behaviour (Focus Score trends by time of day and by session length). This is a strong design point: **self-report sets the starting plan, and observed sessions refine it.**
- Adapted items (reworded, shorter, 5-point scale) are **no longer the validated instrument**. Describe them as "items informed by the MSLQ Time and Study Environment construct (Pintrich et al., 1991)". Do not report subscale scores or norms.
- The MSLQ and LASSI were developed mainly with college students. The wording needs simplifying for high-school users. (Inference: the MSLQ was designed for college courses. This was not directly confirmed in these searches.)

### Gaps
- Copyright and reuse terms for the MSLQ, PPS, Tuckman, MEQ/rMEQ and MCTQ could not be confirmed. The page-fetch block prevented reading the ERIC PDF's copyright page or the MCTQ developers' site. The developer should check the ERIC PDF front matter and contact the authors or rights holders before using any item verbatim. Without permission, write original items.
- Tuckman scale length and licensing are unverified.
- No source was found on validation of these instruments with high-school (13–18) samples specifically.

## Q2. Why not use ADHD screeners (ASRS v1.1, Vanderbilt, SNAP-IV), and how to offer ADHD-friendly features without diagnosis data

### Takeaway
ADHD rating scales are screening aids that clinicians use as one input among several. Guidelines say diagnosis needs a qualified clinician, information from more than one setting and rater, and ruling out other conditions. Several of these scales are not even meant for a teen to fill in about themself: the ASRS is an adult scale, and the Vanderbilt and SNAP-IV are parent- and teacher-report forms validated mostly in elementary-age children. The app should offer "focus-support features" as plain preferences anyone can switch on, never ask about diagnosis or medication, and never output a score that looks like a risk score.

### Cited Findings
- The Harvard NCS background memo states the ASRS Screener v1.1 is a screening scale, **not a diagnostic test**, and that people who screen positive need a diagnostic assessment by a trained clinician. — [Harvard NCS ASRS background memo (rev. 2023)](https://www.hcp.med.harvard.edu/ncs/ftpdir/adhd/background_memo_rev_2023_edit.pdf)
- The NYU licensing page describes the ASRS v1.1 6-question screener as "not a standalone diagnostic tool", meant to help clinicians identify adults at higher risk. It is free to use with attribution, but no modifications other than electronic versions are permitted. — [NYU TOV licensing: ASRS 6Q Screener](https://license.tov.med.nyu.edu/product/asrs6Qscreener)
- The Harvard memo says no approval is needed for individual-level screening if materials acknowledge WHO copyright. A third-party site says the 18-question symptom checklist needs a licence from NYU. These sources differ, so check before any use. — [Harvard NCS memo](https://www.hcp.med.harvard.edu/ncs/ftpdir/adhd/background_memo_rev_2023_edit.pdf); [NYU TOV](https://license.tov.med.nyu.edu/product/asrs6Qscreener)
- Vanderbilt Assessment Scales: validation studies covered the 6–12 age group, with parent and teacher versions. — [Vanderbilt scoring instructions (OUHSC, 2019)](https://soonersuccess.ouhsc.edu/Portals/1024/assets/Vanderbilt%20ADHD%20Rating%20Scales%20Scoring%20Instructions%202019%20.pdf); [Wolraich et al., parent scale psychometrics, PubMed 14602846](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14602846/)
- SNAP-IV psychometric work used parent and teacher ratings of elementary students. The study notes that published cutoffs lack age and gender stratification. — [PMC3623293](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3623293)
- NICE NG87 (2018 text): a diagnosis of ADHD should only be made by a specialist psychiatrist, paediatrician or other appropriately qualified healthcare professional. It should **not** be made solely on rating scales or observational data, and rating scales are "valuable adjuncts". NICE notes later reviews (page shows a July 2026 review, and a 2025 surveillance report proposed updating the diagnosis section with respect to digital technologies). Check the live wording. — [NICE NG87](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87); [NG87 PDF](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/resources/attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-diagnosis-and-management-pdf-1837699732933); [NICE QS39 statement 1](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/qs39/chapter/Quality-statement-1-Confirmation-of-diagnosis)
- AAP 2019 guideline (summarised): the clinician uses DSM-5 criteria with information from parents, teachers and other school personnel. Combining clinical observation and interviews with parent and teacher rating scales increases diagnostic accuracy. — [AAFP summary of AAP 2019 guideline](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2020/0701/p58.html); [Wolraich et al. 2019, Pediatrics, PMC7067282](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7067282)
- CDC: use rating scales **and other sources** to document symptoms and confirm DSM-5 criteria, and rule out other conditions that cause similar symptoms. DSM-5 thresholds are 6 or more symptoms up to age 16 and 5 or more at 17+. — [CDC Clinical Care of ADHD in Children](https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/hcp/clinical-care/index.html)
- ISEF rules: students are prohibited from publishing diagnostic apps on public websites or app stores without appropriate FDA approval. — [Society for Science, Human Participants rules](https://www.societyforscience.org/isef/international-rules/human-participants/) (as summarized in search; verify on page)

### Inferences
- An app that asks ASRS items and shows a result would imitate a clinical screen without the clinical follow-up the instrument's own authors require. It could falsely reassure or falsely alarm a teen. It may also fall under the ISEF ban on diagnostic apps, and it would turn the data into health data (see Q3).
- **Respectful opt-in pattern** (design recommendation, not from a source):
  - Ask about *preferences and needs*, not conditions. Example: "Which of these would help you? (choose any): shorter study blocks, more frequent break reminders, fewer on-screen distractions, gentle nudges when I drift, bigger timers, simple checklists." Present these as "Focus support options", available to everyone.
  - Never ask "Do you have ADHD?", about diagnosis, medication or IEP/504 status. If a user volunteers it, do not store it.
  - Provide a fixed, neutral info link (e.g., "If you're worried about attention, talk to a parent, school counselor or doctor") with no score attached.
  - Avoid wording like "risk", "symptoms", "score" or "you may have…" in the survey output. Keep the Focus Score explicitly about *looking at the screen during this session*, not about the person.
  - Let users change the support options at any time. The plan should adapt from behaviour, not from a label.

### Gaps
- The full text of the AAP 2019 KAS on diagnosis and the current (2026) NICE section 1.3 wording could not be read directly (fetch blocked).
- No authoritative source was found on validation of the ASRS in under-18s. The statement that it is an adult scale rests on the Harvard and NYU descriptions ("adults").

## Q3. Privacy and data protection for minors' health-adjacent data

### Takeaway
Collect the minimum: plan-relevant preferences and schedule only, stored locally where possible, with no diagnosis, health or free-text "status" fields. COPPA applies if the app is aimed at under-13s or knowingly collects their data. The amended rule (compliance from 22 April 2026) adds biometric identifiers such as facial templates and requires a written retention policy. Under GDPR, health data is special-category data. FERPA mostly applies to schools and their contracted vendors, not to a student's independent app.

### Cited Findings
**COPPA (US, under 13)**
- The amended COPPA Rule was published 22 April 2025 and took effect 23 June 2025. Most obligations had a compliance date of 22 April 2026. — [Federal Register 2025-05904](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule); [GovInfo text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-04-22/html/2025-05904.htm)
- The amendments add **biometric identifiers** to "personal information". The FTC did not include data merely derived from facial data, but intends the definition to cover templates such as facial templates. — [Hunton summary](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-publishes-final-coppa-rule-amendments); [Latham & Watkins](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/2025/05/ftc-publishes-updates-to-coppa-rule)
- Operators may keep children's personal information only as long as reasonably necessary for the specific purpose it was collected for, and must publish a written data-retention policy in their privacy notice. — [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-publishes-final-coppa-rule-amendments); [Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2025/05/coppa-rule-ftc-amended-childrens-privacy)

**GDPR (if any EU/UK users)**
- Article 9(1) prohibits processing "data concerning health" (a special category) unless an exception applies. Explicit consent is one exception (Art. 9(2)(a)), and Member States may add conditions for health data (Art. 9(4)). — [legislation.gov.uk Art. 9](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/9/adopted/data.html); [Bird & Bird guide on sensitive data](https://www.twobirds.com/-/media/pdfs/gdpr-pdfs/25--guide-to-the-gdpr--sensitive-data-and-lawful-processing.pdf?la=en)
- Article 8: for information society services offered directly to a child, consent-based processing needs parental authorisation below 16. Member States may lower this to 13 (e.g., Austria 14, Germany 16). — [gdpr-info Art. 8](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-8-gdpr/); [CIPL white paper](https://www.informationpolicycentre.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/cipl_white_paper_-_gdpr_implementation_in_respect_of_childrens_data_and_consent.pdf); [UK ICO children & lawful bases](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/children-and-the-uk-gdpr/how-do-the-lawful-bases-apply-to-children-s-personal-information/)

**FERPA (US school context)**
- FERPA restricts disclosure of personally identifiable information from *education records* held by schools. Edtech vendors receive such data under the "school official" exception only when they perform an institutional function under the school's direct control, use it only for authorized educational purposes and do not redisclose it. — [studentprivacy.ed.gov FAQ: Who is a school official](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/faq/who-school-official-under-ferpa); [CDT analysis](https://cdt.org/insights/commercial-companies-and-ferpas-school-official-exception-a-survey-of-privacy-policies/); [US ED PTAC 2014 guidance](https://tech.ed.gov/files/2014/09/Student-Privacy-and-Online-Educational-Services-February-2014.pdf)
- State student-privacy laws can apply in addition to FERPA. — [CDT 2-pager](https://cdt.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/UPDATED-2021-01-21-Student-Privacy-2pg-Commercial-Edtech-Final.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Data-minimization checklist** (synthesis):
  - Do not collect name, school, date of birth, location or free-text "status". Age can be an age band, or just a "13+" confirmation gate.
  - Do not collect diagnosis, medication, mood or mental-health items. This keeps the data out of GDPR Art. 9 "health data" and avoids ISEF risk categories.
  - Keep webcam processing on-device. Never upload frames, face landmarks or embeddings, and do not persist landmark arrays: store only derived per-session aggregates (e.g., % time looking at screen). This matters because the amended COPPA definition covers facial templates.
  - Store survey answers in localStorage/IndexedDB on the user's device. Offer "Delete my data" and "Skip survey". State a retention period (e.g., "deleted when you reset the app").
  - Make every question optional, with a "Prefer not to say" choice.
  - Put a short plain-language privacy note on the survey's first screen.
- Restricting the public app to 13+ (or requiring parent setup below 13) keeps COPPA out of scope or manageable. Legal advice would be needed for a real launch.
- FERPA likely does not apply unless a school adopts the app and shares student records. This is an inference from the "school official" framework.

### Gaps
- The FTC's own COPPA FAQ and the exact final-rule text on "biometric identifier" could not be fetched. The details come from law-firm summaries plus the Federal Register listing.
- No source was found on how US state laws (e.g., state consumer-health-data laws) would treat "focus/attention" self-report data.

## Q4. ISEF / Society for Science rules on human participants

### Takeaway
Under current ISEF rules, testing a student-built app is exempt from IRB pre-approval **only** when the student (or one adult sponsor/guardian) is the sole tester, there is no health or safety risk and no personal data is collected. Any survey of other people, including surveys about the product's use or opinions, is human-participant research that needs IRB pre-approval, Form 4, a research plan and (for minors) written parental permission that includes a copy of the survey. Because this school prohibits human-participant research, the onboarding survey must stay a product feature. **No real user's survey answers or Focus Scores may be used as project data.** The experiment should use simulated "virtual students" only.

### Cited Findings
- The exempt category covers student-designed inventions, prototypes, computer applications or engineering/design projects where the student is the only person testing, with no health or safety risk. It can extend to a single adult guardian or sponsor as sole tester. IRB pre-approval is required if more than one person tests. A risk assessment is still required. — [ISEF 2026 Rules (SEFI copy)](https://sefi.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/2026-ISEF_Rules.pdf); [Society for Science Human Participants page](https://www.societyforscience.org/isef/international-rules/human-participants/)
- Surveys about potential use, review of the product or opinions about the project count as human-participant research. Students should consult the ISEF Guidelines for Online Survey Consent Procedures before running an online survey. — [Society for Science Human Participants page](https://www.societyforscience.org/isef/international-rules/human-participants/); [ISEF 2026 Rules (SEFI copy)](https://sefi.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/2026-ISEF_Rules.pdf)
- Older and regional summaries tie the product-testing exemption to "NO health hazard, NO personal data collected, and all feedback in direct reference to the product". — [Ohio Academy of Science, 2025 ISEF Rules](https://www.ohiosci.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/2025-ISEF-Rules.pdf); [NYCSEF Human Participants rules](https://k16.cuny.edu/nycsef/wp-content/uploads/sites/11/2019/01/ISEF_Human-Participants-Rules.pdf)
- The 2026–2027 rules add that written parental permission must include a copy of the survey and be obtained **before** a minor accesses an online survey. — [ISEF Rules 2026–2027 (Trine copy)](https://www.trine.edu/science-fair/ISEF%20Rules%202026-2027.pdf); [Society for Science Human Participants page](https://www.societyforscience.org/isef/international-rules/human-participants/)
- Human-participant projects need the Human Participants Form (4), or IRB documentation from a Regulated Research Institution, plus a human-participants research plan. Senior Division projects need Form 4 and IRB pre-approval. — [ISEF 2026 Rules (SEFI copy)](https://sefi.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/2026-ISEF_Rules.pdf); [Ohio Guide to ISEF Forms 2025](https://ssd.ohiosci.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2024/08/Guide-to-ISEF-Forms-2025.pdf); [Trine Form 4](https://www.trine.edu/science-fair/Human%20Participants%20Form%204.pdf); [Texas SEF rules review](https://www.tamucc.edu/education/events/sciencefair/files/resource-junior-senior-isef-rules-review.pdf)
- Students may not publish diagnostic apps on public websites or app stores without FDA approval. Students may not disclose study results or data to participants. — [Society for Science Human Participants page](https://www.societyforscience.org/isef/international-rules/human-participants/)
- Science Buddies' overview of human-subjects rules for fairs. — [Science Buddies](https://www.sciencebuddies.org/science-fair-projects/competitions/human-subjects-regulations)
- Society for Science FAQ (for the developer to check edge cases). — [ISEF Rules FAQ](https://www.societyforscience.org/isef/international-rules/faq/)

### Inferences
- An onboarding survey inside the app is fine **as a product feature**. If anyone other than the student (or a single adult sponsor) uses the app and their answers or Focus Scores are analysed, shown, or summarised in the project, that is human-participant research. That would need IRB approval, Form 4, informed consent and parental permission, which the school prohibits.
- Practical safeguards:
  1. In project documentation, state: "No real user data was collected or analysed. All evaluation used simulated virtual students."
  2. Keep data on-device with no telemetry or analytics, so no data can reach the student even by accident.
  3. Do not ask friends or classmates to try the app and give feedback for the project. Even opinion surveys count.
  4. Self-testing by the student alone is the exempt path. Still complete the risk assessment and confirm with the fair's SRC/IRB, because the webcam and attention features could be judged "health-adjacent".
  5. Simulate survey answers for the virtual students (e.g., sample chronotype, procrastination level and preferred session length from defined distributions) to show how the plan generator responds.
- Keep the app's framing non-diagnostic so it cannot be read as a "diagnostic app" under the FDA clause.

### Gaps
- The official societyforscience.org page could not be read directly. Rule wording comes from search summaries and regional PDF copies, which mix 2025, 2026 and 2026–27 editions. Confirm against the current International Rules and the local SRC.
- The "ISEF Guidelines for Online Survey Consent Procedures" document was not located.

## Q5. Good practice for short in-app questionnaires

### Takeaway
Keep it to about 10–12 mostly closed items (under ~3–4 minutes) with consistent, fully labeled 5-point scales and short plain wording. Make every item optional, add "skip" and "prefer not to say", avoid leading or double-barreled questions, and explain why each block is asked ("so we can suggest study times").

### Cited Findings
- Pew caps its online panel surveys at 15 minutes and budgets each question with points (85 points for 15 minutes). This is based on prior research, and about 80%+ of selected respondents complete. — [Pew Research Decoded, 2022](https://www.pewresearch.org/decoded/2022/12/08/how-we-keep-our-online-surveys-from-running-too-long/)
- Break-off rises with length: one study found more break-off at 25 versus 16 minutes. Industry data suggests abandonment climbs sharply after about 8 minutes. — [SMG white paper](https://smg.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/increasing_response_rates_by_managing_survey_length.pdf); [Survey Research Methods length experiment](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/srm/article/view/8348/7667)
- Evidence on length vs. response rate is mixed. A US Census review found only six studies with significant drops for longer questionnaires and concluded that content may matter as much as length. — [US Census Bureau working paper SM96-05](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/1996/adrm/sm96-05.html)
- A 2021 Austrian experiment found a shorter self-completion questionnaire gave no clear gain in sample composition or data quality. — [Survey Research Methods, "Surely Shorter is Better?"](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/srm/article/view/8348/7667)

### Inferences
- **Suggested survey structure (about 12 items, about 2–3 minutes)**. All items are original wording informed by the cited constructs:
  1. Grade/age band (13–14 / 15–16 / 17–18). Used only for tone and default session lengths. Optional.
  2. Days and times you can usually study (multi-select grid) — practical.
  3. When do you usually feel most alert? (morning / afternoon / evening / late night) — chronotype (rMEQ construct).
  4–5. Usual wake-up time on school days and on days off (time pickers) — MCTQ mid-sleep logic.
  6. Where do you usually study? (desk / bed / kitchen / library / other) — MSLQ Time and Study Environment.
  7. How often does your phone or other apps pull you away while studying? (5-point never–always) — study environment.
  8. Do you already plan your study time ahead? (5-point) — time management.
  9. When studying gets boring or hard, how easy is it to keep going? (5-point) — Effort Regulation.
  10. How often do you start assignments later than you meant to? (5-point never–always) — procrastination (PPS construct).
  11. How long can you usually study before needing a break? (10 / 15 / 25 / 45 / 60+ min) — sets initial block length.
  12. Focus support options (multi-select, see Q2) — opt-in features, no labels.
  13. (Optional) Main goal right now: upcoming test / homework / long-term project / general habits.
