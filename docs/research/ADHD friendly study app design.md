# Build NeuroFocus as a mirror, not a judge

NeuroFocus AI is most useful to students with attention difficulties when it does four things. It makes time visible. It gives short, calm, frequent feedback. It keeps the student as the person who judges their own focus. It plans study with a few well-tested learning-science rules, not with an "AI that learned you." The evidence behind each part is real but uneven. Self-monitoring has large effects on on-task behaviour in small single-case studies ([Reid et al. 2005 via ERIC](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/eric__EJ697216)). Spaced retrieval practice is among the best-supported study methods there are ([Latimier et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)). Purpose-built digital ADHD interventions, however, improve inattention by only about **−0.20 SMD** ([He et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1054831)). NeuroFocus should therefore present itself as a study aid and never as a treatment. The onboarding survey should ask about study habits and preferences in the student's own words, informed by established constructs: time and study environment, effort regulation, procrastination and chronotype. It must never use an ADHD screener, ask about a diagnosis or produce anything that looks like a risk score. Data should stay on the device. After 3–4 sessions the plan should move only partway from population defaults toward the student's own numbers, using a shrinkage formula the student can read. Under ISEF rules, any test by another person, even an opinion survey, counts as human-participant research ([Society for Science](https://www.societyforscience.org/isef/international-rules/human-participants/)). Because the school prohibits that, the project should be evaluated with property-based tests and simulated "virtual students." Those methods can prove that the planner follows its rules and estimates well from noisy data. They cannot prove that students learn more, and the project should say so plainly.

## Time blindness and delay aversion call for visible clocks and minute-scale feedback

The ADHD research that matters most for app design concerns time and reward, not attention in the abstract. A meta-analysis of **55 studies** found "a broad range of timing deficits" in people with ADHD, including significant time-reproduction errors (26 studies, n = 2,364) and small-to-medium time-estimation errors ([Marx et al. 2021](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/456003)). The adult literature is thinner and mixed ([PMC9962130](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9962130/)). A second meta-analysis, of **37 comparisons and 3,763 participants**, found that people with ADHD choose small immediate rewards over larger delayed ones more often than controls. Real rewards roughly doubled this preference ([Marx et al. 2021, J Atten Disord](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/420008)). The gap appears mainly at long delays ([Yu et al. 2015](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/376602)). The design consequence is direct. The session screen should show remaining time as a shrinking bar or ring together with the clock time at which the session ends. Feedback loops should run in minutes, not days. A weekly report on its own arrives far too late to shape behaviour.

Classroom evidence points the same way. A *PLoS ONE* meta-analysis of classroom interventions for children with ADHD symptoms found that consequence-based approaches had much larger within-subject effects (**MSMD = 1.82**) than antecedent-based ones (0.31). Self-regulation interventions were strongest in single-subject designs (**MSMD = 3.61**) ([Gaastra et al. 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4757442/)). Daily behaviour report cards, a frequent-feedback tool, reached **Hedges' g = 1.05** on directly observed outcomes ([PubMed 29135352](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29135352/)). An inexpensive feature with direct ADHD evidence is the if-then plan, also called an implementation intention. Children with ADHD who formed such plans improved Go/No-Go inhibition to the level of non-ADHD peers ([Gawrilow & Gollwitzer 2008](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/08_Gawrilow_Gollwitzer_Implementation_intentions_facilit.pdf)). They also improved working-memory-dependent math and delay of gratification ([Gawrilow et al. 2011, JSCP](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/Gawrilow_Gollwitzer_Oettingen_Executive_Function_JSCP_2011.pdf); [Gawrilow et al. 2011, CTR](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/Gawrilow_Gollwitzer_Oettingen_Delay_2011-2.pdf)). Across the wider population, if-then plans reach **d = 0.65** on goal attainment over 94 tests ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f)). The ADHD studies are small, involve children rather than teenagers and come mostly from one research group, so the feature should be optional rather than mandatory. Still, a session-start prompt costs almost nothing, for example "If I pick up my phone, then I put it face-down and reread the last line."

The most important design decision follows from the self-monitoring literature. Self-regulation interventions for children with ADHD produced combined effects **above 1.0** on on-task behaviour and academic productivity ([Reid, Trout & Schartz 2005](https://www.proquest.com/docview/201228664)). In those studies, though, the student judged their own behaviour. A meta-analysis of 47 single-case studies found larger effects when the accuracy of self-ratings was checked and when students reflected over time. Effects were also larger when the program did *not* use evaluation or rewards, and they were smaller for students with ADHD than for other groups ([Briesch & Briesch 2016](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.17105/SPR45-1.3-18)). NeuroFocus's webcam estimate is a different mechanism that no study has tested. The defensible design keeps the student as the self-monitor. Gentle check-ins ask "Were you on task just now? yes / mostly / no," and the session report shows that self-rating next to the webcam estimate. The camera then serves as an accuracy check, which is the condition linked to larger effects, rather than as a judge. Technology-delivered self-monitoring for high-school students has evidence only from tiny pilots: n = 2 in the I-Connect withdrawal study, and that pilot paired the app with a weekly mentor ([PMC4662407](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4662407)). An optional "share this summary" export, controlled by the student, keeps a human in the loop without building a surveillance dashboard.

Rewards deserve more caution than popular advice gives them. A 2025 review of 22 ADHD games found parent-rated effects from d = −0.55 to 1.26 "without an obvious pattern of advantage for any game element." The largest effects came from the studies at highest risk of bias ([Frontiers in Psychiatry 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1605744/full)). A scoping review called the evidence "scarce and mixed" and flagged poor long-term engagement ([Frontiers in Psychiatry 2022](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.792402/full)). No study was found on streaks for ADHD in either direction. A broken consecutive-day streak is, however, a delayed, all-or-nothing loss signal, which is the opposite of what delay-aversion research recommends. The safer default is informational feedback such as "You refocused 6 times; that is the skill." If streaks appear at all, they should be forgiving ("4 of the last 7 days"). Low Focus Scores should never be shown in red or as failure.

## Accessibility guidelines turn "help users focus" into concrete screen rules

ADHD-specific trials of interface rules do not exist. The W3C's cognitive-accessibility guidance (COGA) and WCAG 2.2 do, however, provide authoritative rules that fit the problem closely. COGA lists "Help Users Focus" as a design objective, with "Limit Interruptions" and "Make Short Critical Paths" as patterns. It notes that people who struggle to keep or regain focus "may have difficulty completing a simple task if there are a lot of distractions and interruptions" ([W3C COGA](https://www.w3.org/tr/coga-usable); [2020 draft](https://www.w3.org/TR/2020/WD-coga-usable-20200717/Overview-mutiple-pages.html)). In COGA's own card-sort, **91.3%** of participants grouped "Limit interruptions" under helping users focus ([COGA TPAC 2021](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/www-archive/2021Oct/att-0007/COGA_Content_Usable_for_TPAC_2021.pdf)). The table maps specific WCAG 2.2 success criteria ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)) to NeuroFocus features.

| Guideline | NeuroFocus rule |
|---|---|
| COGA: Make Short Critical Paths | One large primary action per screen ("Start focus session"). Secondary options sit in a collapsed "More" menu. Starting again after a gap takes one click. |
| COGA: Help Users Focus / Limit Interruptions; WCAG 2.2.4 Interruptions | During a session, "Focus mode" shows only the timer, the task name, the if-then plan and Pause/Stop. Nudges are quiet, never modal and silent by default, with "snooze" and "mute for this session" options. |
| WCAG 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide; 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions | No confetti, pulsing or auto-playing motion. The app respects `prefers-reduced-motion`. The live Focus Score indicator can be hidden. |
| WCAG 2.2.1 Timing Adjustable | Session and break lengths are adjustable, and time limits can be switched off. |
| WCAG 3.2.3 / 3.2.4 / 3.2.6 Consistency | Navigation, button positions and the Help link stay in the same place on every page. |
| WCAG 3.3.7 Redundant Entry; COGA: do not rely on memory | The app remembers the last settings and shows the current task and plan on screen. |
| WCAG 3.1.5 Reading Level | Plain, short sentences. No jargon such as "gaze vector" anywhere in the interface. |

Break design should follow the evidence, not Pomodoro folklore. No randomized trial of the Pomodoro Technique in ADHD or student populations was found. Micro-breaks of 10 minutes or less raise vigour (**d = .36**) and lower fatigue (**d = .35**), but their average effect on performance is small and not significant (d = .16, p = .116). Longer breaks are associated with larger gains ([Albulescu et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36044424/)). One controlled study of university students compared 24-minute blocks plus 6-minute breaks, 12-minute blocks plus 3-minute breaks, and self-chosen breaks. The fixed schedules gave better mood and similar task completion in less time ([Biwer et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/)). A later study from the same group reportedly found the opposite on fatigue and motivation ([PMC12292963](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12292963/)). The common "10–15 minute attention span" claim also has weak support ([Wilson & Korn 2007](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1080/00986280701291291)). NeuroFocus should therefore offer 25 + 5 as "a popular default," offer 12 + 3 and 15 + 5 as presets for students who choose "shorter blocks," and show the break as a visible, guilt-free countdown ending in a gentle "ready to start again?" cue.

Privacy design is part of ADHD-friendliness, not something separate from it. In a 2026 preprint survey of 132 university students, gaze-based attention tracking drew about as negative a reaction as facial emotion recognition. Students worried about being identified as struggling ([Han et al. 2026, arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2602.11074)). Webcam "engagement detection" in classrooms has drawn petitions ([Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/remote-learning-spyware-tracks-student-emotions-1848806568)), and a university was found to have violated a student's privacy by requiring a webcam room scan ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/index%2Ephp/node/142111)). Neurodivergent researchers criticize ADHD technology for framing users as deficient and leaving them out of the design process ([Spiel et al. 2022, CHI](https://dare.uva.nl/id/b5158a6b-ee52-48a7-b4be-772d430bad4a)). Webcam gaze estimation also produces known false positives ([NOVA Lisbon review](https://run.unl.pt/bitstreams/0e03dd03-5be2-4ec9-94cd-9d1f1beff708/download)). NeuroFocus should process frames on the device only and keep a clear camera-on indicator. A one-click "camera off" should leave the timer running. The metric should be labelled honestly ("looking-at-screen estimate"), and the student should be able to correct a session ("I was reading my textbook"). No teacher or parent dashboard should exist by default.

## A twelve-item preference survey replaces any ADHD screener

The onboarding survey should collect what the planner needs and nothing else. Four established constructs map cleanly onto planning decisions. The first two are the Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire's "Time and Study Environment" subscale (8 items) and its "Effort Regulation" subscale (4 items) ([CBE–LSE 2018 table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6234818/table/T2); [ERIC ED338122](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED338122)). The third is procrastination, as captured by Steel's 12-item Pure Procrastination Scale ([PMC5676095](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5676095)). The fourth is chronotype, measured by the 5-item reduced Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire ([PMC2988284](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2988284)) or by the Munich Chronotype Questionnaire's mid-sleep-on-free-days calculation, which needs only reported sleep and wake times ([Univ. Augsburg](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/83600/83600.pdf)). Item wording should not be copied. The LASSI is a commercial product sold per administration ([Buros/UNL](https://marketplace.unl.edu/buros/learning-and-study-strategies-inventory-third-edition.html)), and reuse terms for the MSLQ, PPS, rMEQ and MCTQ could not be confirmed. The project should write original items, describe them as "informed by" each construct, and never call the result a validated scale or report subscale norms. Shortened, reworded items are no longer the validated instrument.

ADHD screeners are not appropriate here, for several separate reasons. The ASRS v1.1 is described by its own developers as a screening scale, "not a standalone diagnostic tool," intended for adults ([Harvard NCS memo](https://www.hcp.med.harvard.edu/ncs/ftpdir/adhd/background_memo_rev_2023_edit.pdf); [NYU licensing](https://license.tov.med.nyu.edu/product/asrs6Qscreener)). The Vanderbilt scales are parent and teacher forms validated in children aged 6–12 ([Wolraich et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14602846/)). NICE says diagnosis must come from a qualified specialist and must not rest solely on rating scales ([NICE NG87](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87)). The AAP and CDC require information from several informants and the exclusion of other conditions ([AAFP summary](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2020/0701/p58.html); [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/hcp/clinical-care/index.html)). ISEF also bars students from publishing diagnostic apps without FDA approval ([Society for Science](https://www.societyforscience.org/isef/international-rules/human-participants/)). A teen-facing ASRS result would imitate a clinical screen without the clinical follow-up that its authors require. NeuroFocus should instead offer "focus support options" that any student can switch on: shorter blocks, more frequent break reminders, fewer on-screen distractions, gentle drift nudges, bigger timers and simple checklists. It should never ask about diagnosis, medication or IEP/504 status, and it should not store such information if a student volunteers it. A neutral link ("If you're worried about attention, talk to a parent, school counselor or doctor") can stand in for any scoring.

The survey should take about 2–3 minutes and use one fully labelled 5-point frequency scale. Each item should ask about one behaviour in neutral wording, with a progress bar, a "Skip for now" option and the ability to retake it from Settings. The evidence on survey length is mixed. Pew caps its panels at 15 minutes ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/decoded/2022/12/08/how-we-keep-our-online-surveys-from-running-too-long/)), break-off rose at 25 versus 16 minutes in one experiment, and shortening a survey did not clearly improve data quality in another ([Survey Research Methods](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/srm/article/view/8348/7667)). For a teenager with attention difficulties during onboarding, however, short is clearly the right call.

The privacy rules point the same way. The amended COPPA Rule, with a compliance date of **22 April 2026**, adds biometric identifiers such as facial templates to "personal information." It also requires a written data-retention policy ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule); [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-publishes-final-coppa-rule-amendments)). GDPR Article 9 treats health data as a special category ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/9/adopted/data.html)), and Article 8 requires parental authorisation below 16, or below a national threshold as low as 13 ([gdpr-info Art. 8](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-8-gdpr/)). FERPA reaches edtech vendors only when they act as a "school official" under a school's direct control ([studentprivacy.ed.gov](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/faq/who-school-official-under-ferpa)), so it most likely does not apply to an independent student app. The practical package for the app follows. It collects no name, school, birth date, location, diagnosis, medication or mood items. A 13+ age gate keeps COPPA out of scope. Survey answers stay in local browser storage. Face landmarks and frames are never uploaded or saved, and only per-session aggregates are kept. The first screen carries a plain-language privacy note, and a "Delete my data" button is always available. A real public launch would still need legal review.

The ISEF rules set the hardest limit. Testing a student-built app is exempt from IRB pre-approval only when the student, or one adult sponsor or guardian, is the sole tester, the app poses no health or safety risk and no personal data is collected. A risk assessment is still required ([ISEF 2026 Rules](https://sefi.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/2026-ISEF_Rules.pdf)). Surveys about use of the product, or opinions about it, count as human-participant research ([Society for Science](https://www.societyforscience.org/isef/international-rules/human-participants/)). The 2026–27 rules also require written parental permission, including a copy of the survey, before a minor opens an online survey ([ISEF 2026–27 Rules](https://www.trine.edu/science-fair/ISEF%20Rules%202026-2027.pdf)). The school prohibits human-participant research, so the onboarding survey can exist only as a product feature. No friend or classmate may try the app for the project, and no real user's answers or Focus Scores may appear as project data. The developer should still confirm with the fair's SRC that a webcam attention feature does not count as "health-adjacent." These rule summaries come from regional PDF copies and search summaries, not from the live Society for Science page, so they need a check against the current International Rules.

## Shrinkage, spacing and slack make three sessions enough for an honest plan

Three or four sessions give three or four observations per student. That is too few to train or validate any machine-learning model. The data-driven spaced-repetition systems that do personalize were trained on millions of review traces ([Settles & Meeder 2016](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/P16-1174); [Tabibian et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1815156116)). The defensible label is therefore "rule-based personalization with statistical shrinkage." The statistical core is the idea Efron and Morris made famous with baseball: early-season averages pulled toward the group mean predict the rest of the season better than the raw averages do ([Efron & Morris 1975](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1975.10479864); [Gelman et al., BDA3](http://www.stat.columbia.edu/~gelman/book/)). For block length the formula is **personal = w × (median focus streak) + (1 − w) × 25 min, with w = n / (n + k)**. Here n is the number of sessions and k ≈ 4. With n = 3, w ≈ 0.43, so the estimate stays near the default. With n = 12, w = 0.75. The result is rounded to 5 minutes and clamped to 10–50 minutes. The median is used because streaks are right-skewed. The app can display this calculation word for word. Time-of-day bands use the same pattern: (n_b × band mean + k × overall mean) / (n_b + k). A band is called "better" only when it has at least 3 sessions and the shrunk difference passes a set threshold. Without those guards the app would report regression to the mean as if it were insight ([Barnett et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyh299)). The value k ≈ 4 and the session thresholds are design heuristics, not published values. Measuring the test–retest reliability of the developer's own focus streaks would give k an empirical basis.

The schedule itself rests on stronger evidence. Practice testing and distributed practice are the two techniques rated "high utility," while rereading and highlighting rate "low" ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)). Spaced retrieval beats massed retrieval with **g = 0.74**, and expanding and equal-gap schedules do not differ reliably (g = 0.034) ([Latimier et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)). The planner can therefore use equal gaps, which are easier to explain. Gap size can follow the "ridgeline" study of more than 1,350 learners. The best gap is about **20–40% of the time until a test one week away**, falling to about 5–10% for a test a year away, and a gap that is too long costs less than one that is too short ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)). Interleaving has a moderate average effect (**g = 0.42**) with high heterogeneity. It helps when categories are similar and complex, and it can hurt for expository text (g = −0.39 in one subgroup) ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)). Planning needs slack because students systematically underestimate how long academic work takes ([Buehler et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366)). Time management relates more strongly to wellbeing than to performance ([Aeon et al. 2021](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:plo:pone00:0245066)), so the app should promise less stress and better follow-through, not higher grades.

| Plan decision | Rule | "Why?" text shown to student |
|---|---|---|
| Who chooses | The student picks study days, time windows, subjects/topics and deadlines. The planner only arranges blocks inside those windows. | "You chose these times. I only fit blocks into them." |
| Block length | Shrinkage formula above. A "shorter blocks" option starts from 12–15 min. | "Default 25 min, moved 43% toward your typical 18-min streak (3 sessions, confidence: low)." |
| Breaks | 5 min after each block; 15–20 min after 3–4 blocks | "Breaks reduce fatigue (Albulescu 2022)." |
| New material | First study block placed early. New material finished 1–2 days before the deadline. | "Leaves the last days for practice tests." |
| Reviews | At least 2–3 retrieval blocks per topic, gap ≈ 0.2–0.4 × days to deadline, minimum 1 day, longer gap if the ideal one doesn't fit | "Spacing reviews out helps memory last (Cepeda 2008)." |
| Review type | Self-test, practice questions or blurt-recall; never "reread" | "Testing yourself beats rereading (Dunlosky 2013)." |
| Mixing | Interleave only similar problem types within one subject | "Mixing similar problem types helps; mixing unrelated subjects doesn't." |
| Slot ranking | Hardest or newest work goes in the highest-ranked slot (survey alertness answer plus shrunk measured focus) | "You said evenings feel best; not enough data yet to confirm." |
| Buffer | Plan fills ≤70–80% of the time the student says is available | "Plans usually take longer than expected, so I left catch-up time." |
| Block wording | Each block written as an implementation intention | "Tue 17:00, at my desk, 2 × 25 min Chemistry practice questions." |

Time of day should stay a soft preference. Adolescents drift toward eveningness ([Roenneberg et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2004.11.039)), and adolescents show some evidence of a synchrony effect ([Goldstein et al. 2007](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/time-of-day-intellectual-performance-and-behavioral-problems-in-m/)). Counter-evidence also exists ([Behav Sci 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/14/4/310)), and no adolescent meta-analysis of synchrony was found. After the plan starts, the app can keep adapting with a simple rule. If at least 2 of the last 3 blocks were completed above threshold, the block lengthens by 5 minutes. If at least 2 were abandoned, it shortens by 5 minutes. A Thompson-sampling bandit over 15/20/25/35-minute options is a more ambitious alternative ([Russo et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.02038)), but it needs dozens of sessions to converge, so over a few weeks it acts mostly as structured exploration. The Focus Score cannot see paper study, so the plan needs an "off-screen study" mode. Otherwise every textbook block will look like distraction.

## Simulated virtual students can verify the planner but not the learning

Without human participants, the project can rigorously answer two engineering questions. The first is whether the planner always obeys its rules. The second is whether shrinkage estimates a student's needs better than the alternatives when data is noisy. The first question calls for property-based testing (Hypothesis in Python, fast-check in JavaScript) over about **10,000 randomized cases**. Each case varies availability windows, subjects, deadlines and synthetic session histories. Every generated plan must pass a set of checks. No block falls outside the student's chosen windows, and no blocks overlap. Every topic is first studied before its deadline, and each topic gets at least 2 retrieval reviews when time allows. Review gaps stay within the target fraction or the nearest feasible longer gap. Planned time stays at or below 80% of available time, blocks stay within 10–50 minutes, and long breaks appear where they should. Identical inputs produce identical outputs. A transparency audit then confirms that every decision carries a readable reason string that cites its rule and source. The planner should also be compared against two baselines, a "cram before the deadline" plan and a fixed 25-minute plan with no personalization. The comparison uses mean review gap divided by days to deadline, reviews per topic, and slack.

The second question calls for a Monte Carlo experiment. The experiment creates virtual students with known true median streaks drawn from about 10–45 minutes and adds session-to-session noise. It generates 3, 4, 8 and 12 sessions for each student and compares the mean absolute error of three estimators: the population default, the raw personal median, and the shrinkage estimate. The expected result follows from the Efron–Morris logic. Shrinkage should win at small n and converge toward the raw median as n grows. The sensitivity analysis varies k and the noise level. Survey answers for the virtual students should also be simulated, by sampling chronotype, procrastination level and preferred block length from defined distributions, to show that each answer moves the plan as the rules intend. An optional third layer feeds the spaced and crammed schedules into a published forgetting model such as half-life regression ([Settles & Meeder 2016](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/P16-1174)). This layer is labelled strictly as a model-based illustration.

The limitation statement should be explicit, and it adds credibility rather than costing it. No real user data was collected or analysed, and all evaluation used simulated virtual students. The webcam metric has no validation against learning or genuine attention. Even purpose-built digital interventions show small inattention effects ([He et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1054831)), and a review of 26 systematic reviews called the field "inconclusive due to low evidence quality" ([BMC Psychiatry 2025](https://doaj.org/article/789ef3c13fd143468097858bb49f61e5)). Effects on grades, retention or wellbeing would require a future study with IRB approval, informed consent and parental permission.

## Conclusion

The research changes what "ADHD-friendly" should mean for this project. It is less about adding stimulation, through points, badges and streaks, and more about removing time-related and evaluative pressure. That means time the student can see, feedback that arrives within minutes, a camera that checks the student's own judgment instead of replacing it, and a plan that shows its reasoning and admits when it is unsure. These choices are also the ones that keep the project inside ISEF rules and privacy law. Asking about preferences instead of diagnosis, keeping processing on the device and labelling the metric honestly all serve the student and the rules at the same time, so good design and compliance do not have to be traded against each other.

The strongest scientific claim available to the project is not "NeuroFocus improves focus." It is narrower and easier to defend: "From 3–4 noisy sessions, shrinkage personalization provably beats both a fixed default and naive personalization on simulated students, and every plan satisfies evidence-derived scheduling constraints in 100% of 10,000 randomized cases." That claim is testable now, without participants, and it sets up a clear follow-up study. The study would measure the reliability of focus streaks and the agreement between self-ratings and the webcam once human-participant work is approved. The number k and the session thresholds are the project's weakest assumptions, and they are exactly what such a study would measure.
