# Nudge softly, then suggest rest honestly

NeuroFocus AI can bring a drifting student back without a "jump scare" by changing how fast a sound starts, not only how loud it is. Startle depends mainly on a near-instant rise time combined with high intensity, especially broadband noise. Even very intense sounds fail to trigger a blink or body jerk when the onset is slow enough ([Taylor & Francis review chapter](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/9781317783299/chapters/10.4324/9781315807355-6)). The safe recipe is a quiet, sine-based, two-note falling chime with an **80–150 ms fade-in**, lasting under about 1.2 s in total. It should follow a visual cue, never escalate in loudness, and stay off by default until the student opts in, because sound intolerance is common among neurodivergent users. For tiredness, the webcam can approximate **PERCLOS**, the share of each minute the eyes are mostly closed. PERCLOS is the best-validated ocular drowsiness measure ([FHWA Tech Brief 1998](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/113)), and the app can add long blinks and yawns to it. It should suggest a 3–6 minute break only when two signs agree and the student can dismiss the suggestion. Every threshold below is calibrated per user, and all of them come from driving and lab research rather than from studying at a desk. Two honest limits follow. The app shows "signs you might be tired," never a measurement of fatigue. And no study has tested distraction-triggered audio cues, so the ISEF project can verify how the cues are engineered but cannot claim they improve focus.

## Rise time, not volume alone, separates a nudge from a startle

The acoustic startle literature points to one lever above all others: how fast a sound reaches full level. Human startle studies typically use **95–105 dB white noise** with near-instant onsets. In one such study, varying the rise time from 0 to 240 ms changed the blink-startle response ([Psychonomic/Springer PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03205275.pdf)). Animal work shows the same pattern. In rats, a short rise time is "a crucial factor" for startle ([Acta Neurobiologiae Experimentalis](https://ane.pl/index.php/ane/article/download/1451/1451)). In dolphins, startle to noise fell as rise time grew from 2 to 100 ms, and models put full suppression at about **141–220 ms** ([J. Exp. Biol.](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article-pdf/1982528/jeb208470.pdf)). Those dolphin numbers come from another species and are modelled, so they set a direction, not a human threshold. Patterson's work on auditory warnings reportedly treats a slower onset of about **50 ms** as less startling ([Patterson 1990](https://www.pdn.cam.ac.uk/system/files/documents/AuditoryWarningsAtWork_RSoc1990.pdf)). That figure reached the research notes secondhand because the original could not be fetched. The standard human methods guideline ([Blumenthal et al. 2005](https://www.scilit.net/publications/1cf3a7bb59abaa293b92df62a19c520a)) was also not read in full.

A gentle cue should also *sound* unurgent. Edworthy and colleagues showed that fundamental frequency, harmonic content, amplitude envelope and pulse rate have "clear and consistent effects on perceived urgency," and that these effects are predictable enough to engineer ([Edworthy et al. 1991](https://researchportal.plymouth.ac.uk/en/publications/improving-auditory-warning-design-relationship-between-warning-so/); [Hellier et al. 1993](https://researchportal.plymouth.ac.uk/en/publications/improving-auditory-warning-design-quantifying-and-predicting-the-/)). Patterson's aviation guidelines set warning level at roughly **15–25 dB above the masked threshold** of the background noise, not at maximum ([CAA 82017 via IOA](https://www.ioa.org.uk/system/files/publications/RD%20PATTERSON%20GUIDELINES%20FOR%20THE%20DESIGN%20OF%20AUDITORY%20WARNING%20SOUNDS.pdf)). Taken together, the low-urgency settings are a low pitch, few harmonics, a rounded envelope, a falling contour and no fast repetition. The papers give the direction of each effect. The specific Hz values in the table are engineering choices based on that direction.

| Parameter | Default | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Waveform | `sine`, optional 2nd harmonic at −12 dB; no noise, square or sawtooth | Fewer harmonics lower urgency (Edworthy, direction) |
| Pitch | Two-note falling chime, ~660 Hz → 523 Hz (E5→C5), kept within 300–1000 Hz | Lower pitch and falling contour lower urgency (inference) |
| Attack | 80–150 ms via `linearRampToValueAtTime`, never 0 ms (0 ms also clicks in Web Audio) | Slower rise reduces startle (rise-time literature) |
| Release | 300–600 ms exponential decay; notes 250–400 ms; whole cue < 1.2 s | Stays far under the WCAG 3 s limit |
| Level | Master `GainNode` ~0.05–0.15 (≈ −26 to −16 dBFS peak); never auto-raised | Moderate level above background, not maximum (Patterson) |

## Escalate by meaning, cap at three tiers, default to silence

The cue should also stop working on the student before the student stops trusting it. A sensible ladder escalates by *meaning* rather than volume. Tier 0 is a soft screen-edge glow after about 20–30 s of detected drift. Tier 1 is the chime if the drift continues for about 30 s more. Tier 2, which is opt-in, is a quiet synthesized line such as "Ready to jump back in?" (`speechSynthesis` rate ~0.9, volume ~0.6) after another minute. After that the app stops. Audio cues should be **at least 2–3 minutes apart and capped at about 6–10 per hour**, and the cooldown should double after two dismissals in a row, because startle and orienting responses habituate and constant repetition only adds annoyance. These timings are reasoned defaults. No study supplied them, and no study compared earcons with speech for refocusing students. Both are gaps the project should state openly.

Silence is the right default because many users find sound painful. A 2021 meta-analysis put hyperacusis in autism at about **41% by questionnaire and 27% by objective measures**, and ADHD is a common co-occurring condition in that literature ([ENT & Audiology News](https://entandaudiologynews.com/features/audiology-features/post/hyperacusis-and-autism-spectrum-disorder); [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7855558)). Sound sensitivity in ADHD itself is under-studied; a 30-versus-30 pilot in children exists, but its results were not reviewed ([IJERPH 2020](https://mdpi.com/1660-4601/17/9/3045)). WCAG SC 1.4.2 (Level A) requires that any audio playing automatically for more than 3 s has a pause or stop control or a volume control independent of system volume ([W3C Understanding 1.4.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/audio-control.html); [Deque axe rule](https://dequeuniversity.com/rules/axe/4.8/no-autoplay-audio)). A sub-1.2 s cue plus an in-app mute and slider meets this requirement two ways over. First run should therefore be visual-only, with audio offered during onboarding as "gentle chime" or "chime + voice."

The case that prompts help at all rests on small studies. In an ABAB study of three elementary students, a vibrating interval prompt (MotivAider) raised on-task behaviour from **55% to over 90%** of observed intervals ([Amato-Zech et al. 2006](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c4287-c19fb45625e5a546257c9895b4872a3c255de4ff8f4adbc7a153311d3269863c3)). High-school students with ADHD in a biology class completed more independent work with tactile-cued self-monitoring ([Morrison et al. 2014](https://clutejournals.com/index.php/TLC/article/view/8856)). Two theses found that student-operated prompting beat teacher-operated prompting ([ETSU 3343](https://dc.etsu.edu/etd/3343); [ETSU 3450](https://dc.etsu.edu/etd/3450)). All of these prompts were tactile and fixed-interval. None was triggered by a webcam or delivered by audio. The design lesson is to frame the chime as a cue for the student's own "Am I on task?" check, which the student configures, rather than as a reprimand.

## PERCLOS and long blinks can flag tiredness; blink rate cannot

The FHWA/NHTSA work by Dinges and colleagues found PERCLOS, the proportion of a minute with the eyes at least 80% closed, to be the most reliable and valid of the drowsiness measures it tested ([FHWA Tech Brief](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/113); [DOT HS 808 762](https://trid.trb.org/View/647942)). Blink duration, slow lid reopening and lid-closure speed are also strong indicators, but they vary widely between individuals ([Schleicher et al. 2008](https://www.ingentaconnect.com/content/10.1080/00140130701817062)). A webcam approximates eye closure with the eye aspect ratio (EAR) computed from facial landmarks, which sits roughly constant when the eye is open and drops toward zero in a blink. The baseline "closed" threshold is **EAR < 0.2** ([Soukupová & Čech 2016](https://vision.fe.uni-lj.si/cvww2016/proceedings/papers/05.pdf)). Fixed EAR and mouth-aspect-ratio thresholds generalise poorly. Personalised thresholds improve accuracy, and one system counts a yawn only when the mouth stays open for more than 15 consecutive frames, which filters out speech ([PeerJ CS 2022](https://peerj.com/articles/cs-943); [arXiv 2604.22479](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.22479)). A 2023 review warns that PERCLOS definitions vary and that PERCLOS alone may lack sensitivity in real settings, so it works best combined with other measures ([Abe 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10108649/)).

One popular sign should be dropped. A low blink rate does not reliably indicate fatigue at a screen. A controlled study found **14.9 vs 13.6 blinks/min** for screen versus paper (p = 0.58), with more incomplete blinks on screen ([PubMed 24413278](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24413278/)). Concentration also suppresses blinking, so a blink-rate rule would fire on exactly the focused state the app exists to protect. The desk setting adds its own false positives. Looking down at notes lowers EAR without the eyes closing, and talking, laughing or eating opens the mouth. Glasses, lighting and camera angle shift every baseline.

| Signal | Default rule | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Calibration | 30–60 s at session start; closed = own open-eye EAR × 0.5–0.6 (fallback EAR < 0.2; or `eyeBlink` blendshape > 0.5) | Per-user thresholds generalise better |
| PERCLOS | Fraction of closed frames over a rolling 60 s; flag when > ~0.15 for ≥ 2 consecutive minutes | Per-minute definition cited; the 0.15 cut-off is driving practice and untested at a desk |
| Long blinks | Closure 0.4–2 s; flag at ≥ 3/min or median duration ≥ 30% above own baseline; ≥ 2 s = eyes-closed event | Direction from Schleicher; numbers are inference |
| Yawn | Mouth open ≥ 2× baseline (or `jawOpen` > 0.6) for ≥ 1.5 s / > 15 frames; flag at ≥ 2 in 10 min | Frame rule cited; rest inference |
| Head pose | Exclude frames with > ~20° downward pitch from eye statistics; nods are supporting evidence only | Desk-specific inference |
| Blink rate | Ignore | Screen vs paper study |
| Decision | Suggest a break when ≥ 2 independent signs agree within 5 min, or after ~30 min of continuous work; ≥ 15 min cooldown; dismissal snoozes 20 min | Combining measures (Abe 2023) |

## Offer a short break as a choice, with the reason shown

Once tiredness signs agree, the break itself has reasonable evidence behind it. Sustained attention is effortful, and performance declines with time on task ([Warm et al. 2008](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1518%2F001872008X312152)). A meta-analysis of 22 samples (N = 2,335) found that micro-breaks of 10 minutes or less raised vigour (**d = 0.36**) and reduced fatigue (**d = 0.35**). Their overall performance benefit was small and not significant (d = 0.16, p = .116), and longer breaks gave larger gains ([Albulescu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9432722/)). Among students, fixed breaks of 6 minutes after every 24 minutes improved mood and finished the same work in less time than self-chosen breaks ([Biwer et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/)). A later study from the same group, however, found fatigue rising faster under Pomodoro-style breaks than under self-regulated or "Flowtime" breaks ([Smits et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12292963/)). The defensible design is therefore a hybrid. A gentle time-based suggestion appears around 25–30 minutes, a sign-based suggestion appears when the webcam evidence agrees, the default break is **5 minutes (range 3–10)**, and a longer 10–15 minute break is offered after about 90 minutes. That last figure follows the direction of the meta-regression and is not a cited value.

The wording carries the ethics. The prompt should state the reason and leave the choice with the student: "Looks like you might be getting tired (more long blinks than usual). Want a 5-minute break?" with [Take a break] [Not now] [Snooze]. It should never say "You are tired," "fatigue detected" or anything clinical. Showing the reason lets the student overrule a false positive caused by glasses, glare or reading notes, which matters because ocular measures vary greatly between people ([Schleicher et al. 2008](https://www.ingentaconnect.com/content/10.1080/00140130701817062)). The surrounding controls, carried over from the app's broader privacy design, include an opt-in camera, on-device processing, a visible camera-on indicator, one-click pause, adjustable sensitivity and no identifiable logs. No study of break-prompt wording for students with ADHD was found, so this copy rests on general autonomy-support principles.

## Conclusion

The central design insight is that "gentle" can be engineered. A slow attack, low pitch, sine timbre, falling contour, modest level and capped repetition are each parameters with a documented direction of effect. NeuroFocus can therefore show *why* its chime is not a jump scare without testing it on anyone. That fits the project's no-human-participants constraint. The cue generator can be verified by measuring its own output: confirm that rise time stays at 80 ms or more, total duration stays under 1.2 s, peak gain never exceeds the user's setting, and the cooldowns hold across thousands of simulated drift sequences.

The fatigue side is where honesty matters most. Every threshold in the table comes from drivers or lab tasks, so the project's defensible claim is narrow: the app combines validated *kinds* of signals, calibrates them per user, and requires agreement before it speaks. That rule is built to keep false alarms down, but no data here shows how many it prevents. Whether a PERCLOS of 0.15 means "tired" for a teenager reading at a desk, and whether a webcam-triggered chime beats a fixed-interval one, are open empirical questions. They belong to a future IRB-approved study, not to the claims of this one.
